Failed closing · Ontario

The buyer didn’t close. What happens now?

It is the week nobody plans for. The money did not arrive, your own purchase may depend on it, and the advice you are getting is to sue. Before you commit to that, it is worth knowing what the whole road looks like.

What is probably happening

Most failed closings in this market are not bad faith. They are financing. The lender appraised below the purchase price, the buyer is suddenly short the difference, and nobody involved has that cash sitting in an account. The appraisal gap has become one of the most common reasons deals die in Ontario, and it produces a buyer who is not refusing to close so much as unable to.

That distinction matters more than it sounds, because it changes what is actually recoverable.

The two roads

The usual one

You instruct a litigator. A claim is issued. The buyer defends, and if they had no money to close they have no money for a judgment either.

Eighteen months to two years. Tens of thousands per side. The property sits, or you resell into whatever market exists by then.

You may win. A judgment is a claim against someone, not money in your account, and collecting it is a second job.

The other one

Both sides in a room within weeks, with someone neutral who has done this before.

The conversation is not who breached. It is what can be salvaged: an extension with a price adjustment, a larger non-refundable deposit, a vendor take-back for the shortfall, an assignment to a replacement buyer, or a clean mutual release with a number attached.

One fixed fee, split. Private. Nothing filed, nothing searchable.

Neither road is automatically right. But almost nobody gets told the second one exists, and by the time they hear about it the legal costs have already changed the maths.

The questions worth answering before you decide

  • Does the buyer actually have assets, or would a judgment be a piece of paper?
  • What does the property realistically sell for now, and how does that compare to your contract price?
  • What is it costing you to carry it in the meantime?
  • Is your own purchase depending on this closing?
  • How much of the deposit is in trust, and on what terms?
Worth knowing

In Ontario, a deposit and a claim for damages are two different things, and the answers turn on your specific agreement and what actually happened. That is a question for your own lawyer, and you should ask them. What this page is for is the part they are not retained to tell you: what each road costs, how long it takes, and what you are likely to be left holding at the end of it.
