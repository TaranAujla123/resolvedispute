Case notes

What the law actually says.

People in a dispute are usually working from something they were told, something they read, or something they assume. These are 18 of the decisions that actually govern the matters we resolve, set out plainly: the facts, what the court decided, and the rule that came out of it.

This is information, not advice. Each note describes a decided case. It does not tell you what any of it means for your situation, because that depends on facts I have not seen and on law that may have moved since. Read it, apply your own judgement, and take the question to your own lawyer.

Every case here links to the full decision. Read the original if anything turns on it.

Real estate

Failed closings, deposits, mortgage enforcement and default interest.

Semelhago v. Paramadevan
[1996] 2 SCR 415
What happened

A buyer agreed to purchase a house being built in Toronto and intended to finance it partly by selling his existing home. The deal fell apart. He sued and elected damages rather than specific performance. Values had risen sharply in the meantime.

What the court decided

The Supreme Court held that specific performance should not be granted as a matter of course simply because the subject of the contract is land. A plaintiff must show that the property has qualities making it sufficiently unique that damages would not be an adequate substitute.

The rule

Land is no longer presumed unique. Specific performance requires evidence that damages cannot do the job.

Why it comes up in mediation

A party who says “I will simply force them to close” may be describing a remedy they are not entitled to. That single point often changes what each side thinks it can hold out for.

Southcott Estates Inc. v. Toronto Catholic District School Board
2012 SCC 51
What happened

A single-purpose development company contracted to buy school board land. The board did not complete. Southcott sued for specific performance and, in the alternative, damages. It made no attempt to buy any comparable property while the litigation ran.

What the court decided

A plaintiff seeking specific performance is not relieved of the ordinary duty to mitigate unless it shows a substantial justification for that position. Southcott had not established the land was unique, so its failure to mitigate was fatal. It recovered nominal damages of $1.

The rule

Claiming specific performance does not suspend the duty to mitigate. A plaintiff who sits still may recover nothing even though the other side breached.

Why it comes up in mediation

This is the case that reality-tests a confident claimant. Being right about the breach and being compensated for it are different questions, and the gap between them is decided by what the wronged party did next.

Redstone Enterprises Ltd. v. Simple Technology Inc.
2017 ONCA 282
What happened

A purchaser agreed to buy a warehouse for $10.255 million and paid a deposit of $750,000. It did not close. It asked the court for relief from forfeiture of the deposit, pointing out that the vendor had proved no actual loss.

What the court decided

The Court of Appeal restored the forfeiture of the full deposit. A deposit stands as a guarantee of performance and is generally forfeited on the purchaser's default. Relief from forfeiture is available only where the forfeiture would be unconscionable, which is an exceptional standard, not simply a disproportionate one.

The rule

A deposit is forfeited on default even where the seller suffers no provable loss. Relief from forfeiture is rare.

Why it comes up in mediation

Purchasers frequently arrive believing a large deposit must come back if the seller was not actually harmed. It usually does not, and knowing that before the negotiation starts changes the shape of it.

Azzarello v. Shawqi
2019 ONCA 820
What happened

A purchaser failed to close a $1.55 million residential purchase and forfeited a $75,000 deposit. The vendors resold at a loss and also claimed damages for the shortfall, arguing they should keep the deposit on top.

What the court decided

The deposit is forfeited on repudiation, but where the vendor also claims damages the deposit is credited against the damages award. The vendor is made whole, not better than whole.

The rule

Deposit plus full damages is not available. The deposit is applied against proven loss.

Why it comes up in mediation

Most failed-closing negotiations turn on a single number. This case tells both sides how that number is actually built, which usually narrows the gap considerably.

Scott v. Forjani
2022 ONCA 30
What happened

A purchaser agreed to buy a property for $1,450,000 with a closing roughly thirteen months out. She refused to close, alleging undisclosed latent defects and failure to maintain. The motion judge found she had breached the agreement and acted in bad faith, and ordered the deposit forfeited. The vendors relisted and eventually sold for $1,120,000.

What the court decided

Damages were assessed on the difference between the contract price and the price actually achieved on resale, producing an award of $330,000. Challenges to whether the vendors could have obtained more on resale required evidence.

The rule

In a falling market the breaching buyer carries the difference between the contract price and the realistic resale price, and arguments about mitigation have to be proved, not asserted.

Why it comes up in mediation

Exactly the arithmetic behind most 2022 to 2026 failed closings. The longer the parties argue, the further the resale market can move, and the number grows while they do.

Krayzel Corp. v. Equitable Trust Co.
2016 SCC 18
What happened

A commercial mortgage was renewed on terms under which a lower rate applied only if payments were made on time, with a higher rate applying otherwise. The borrower argued the arrangement offended section 8 of the federal Interest Act.

What the court decided

Section 8 prohibits any arrangement whose effect is to increase the charge on arrears beyond the rate payable on principal not in arrears. The court looks at substance rather than the label. A provision framed as a discount for good behaviour can still be caught.

The rule

Default interest arrangements are tested by effect, not by drafting. Calling an increase a bonus, discount or incentive does not save it.

Why it comes up in mediation

Lenders and borrowers often negotiate off a payout figure that includes an unenforceable component. Establishing what is actually owed is usually the first honest step in a mortgage file.

Walia v. 2155982 Ontario Inc.
2020 ONCA 493
What happened

A short-term mortgage carried interest at 12 percent, with a term providing that on default or on failure to pay at maturity the rate would rise to 21 percent. The lender sued to enforce it.

What the court decided

The Court of Appeal upheld the finding that the increase was unenforceable under section 8 of the Interest Act, applying Krayzel. The rate could not rise because of the default.

The rule

An Ontario mortgage cannot impose a higher rate on arrears than on principal not in arrears.

Why it comes up in mediation

Private and secondary mortgage files often carry exactly this structure. The amount in dispute can shrink materially once the enforceable rate is identified.

Business

Contracts, good faith, shareholders and oppression, commercial leases, and the realistic shape of litigation.

Sattva Capital Corp. v. Creston Moly Corp.
2014 SCC 53
What happened

Two sophisticated parties disagreed over which date governed the valuation of shares payable as a finder's fee. Each read the same finder's fee agreement differently.

What the court decided

Contractual interpretation reads the agreement as a whole, giving the words their ordinary and grammatical meaning consistent with the surrounding circumstances known to the parties when the contract was formed. Surrounding circumstances inform the words but cannot overwhelm them.

The rule

Meaning comes from the words read in their commercial context, not from the words in isolation and not from what a party later wishes had been agreed.

Why it comes up in mediation

Two competent parties can hold genuinely different readings of the same clause without either acting in bad faith. Accepting that is often what makes a commercial negotiation possible at all.

Bhasin v. Hrynew
2014 SCC 71
What happened

An enrolment director's dealership agreement renewed automatically unless notice was given. The other party had been working with a competitor to take over his agency while telling him otherwise, then gave notice of non-renewal.

What the court decided

Good faith contractual performance is an organizing principle of the common law, and from it flows a duty of honest performance: parties must not lie to or knowingly mislead each other about matters directly linked to performance of the contract.

The rule

A party may exercise its contractual rights, but it may not lie about how or whether it will do so.

Why it comes up in mediation

Ongoing relationships, distribution, franchise, partnership and shareholder arrangements are where this bites. The contractual right can be clear and the manner of its exercise still create exposure.

C.M. Callow Inc. v. Zollinger
2020 SCC 45
What happened

Condominium corporations decided to terminate a winter maintenance contract but said nothing for months, while allowing the contractor to believe renewal was likely and to perform free extra work.

What the court decided

The duty of honest performance was breached. A party need not disclose its intentions, but it cannot knowingly mislead, including through active communications, half-truths, omissions or silence that creates a false impression.

The rule

Silence can breach the duty of honest performance where it is calculated to mislead about the exercise of a contractual right.

Why it comes up in mediation

A great many commercial disputes are really about how something was done rather than whether it could be done. This case gives that grievance a legal name, which usually has to be acknowledged before anything settles.

Wastech Services Ltd. v. Greater Vancouver Sewerage and Drainage District
2021 SCC 7
What happened

A long-term waste transportation contract gave the authority absolute discretion over waste allocation among facilities. It allocated in a way that made the contractor's target operating ratio unachievable in that year.

What the court decided

Contractual discretion must be exercised reasonably, meaning in a manner connected to the purposes for which the discretion was granted. On these facts the exercise was consistent with those purposes, so there was no breach. Good faith does not require a party to subordinate its own interests.

The rule

Discretion is bounded by the purpose it was given for. It is not unlimited, and it is not a duty to act selflessly either.

Why it comes up in mediation

Answers the party who says the contract gives them absolute discretion, and equally the party who assumes good faith means their counterpart must look after them. Both positions get narrowed.

BCE Inc. v. 1976 Debentureholders
2008 SCC 69
What happened

Debentureholders opposed a leveraged buyout that would leave their debentures trading lower, arguing the transaction was oppressive to them.

What the court decided

The oppression remedy asks two questions: does the evidence support the reasonable expectation asserted by the claimant, and does the evidence establish that the expectation was violated by conduct that was oppressive, unfairly prejudicial, or unfairly disregarded a relevant interest.

The rule

Oppression is measured against reasonable expectations, established by things like commercial practice, the nature of the corporation, the relationship between the parties and past dealings.

Why it comes up in mediation

“Reasonable expectations” is already the language of a negotiation. In shareholder and family-company disputes it moves the conversation from who is entitled to what, to what everyone actually understood they were building.

Wilson v. Alharayeri
2017 SCC 39
What happened

A former president and significant shareholder was diluted when the board declined to convert his preferred shares while converting another director's. He sought a personal order against two directors rather than against the corporation.

What the court decided

Directors can be held personally liable under the oppression remedy where they are implicated in the oppressive conduct and personal liability is fit in all the circumstances, including where they accrued a personal benefit or exercised control.

The rule

The corporate form is not automatic protection in an oppression claim.

Why it comes up in mediation

Negotiations change when a director discovers the exposure may not stop at the company. It is one of the few points that reliably moves an entrenched position.

Highway Properties Ltd. v. Kelly, Douglas and Co.
[1971] SCR 562
What happened

A tenant in a new shopping centre abandoned the premises well before the end of a fifteen-year lease. The centre failed. The landlord re-entered and sued, including for prospective loss over the remaining term.

What the court decided

A commercial lease is a contract as well as a conveyance. A landlord faced with repudiation may terminate, notify the tenant it is reletting on the tenant's account, and claim damages for losses over the unexpired term, provided it mitigates.

The rule

Ordinary contract remedies, including prospective damages for the balance of the term, are available to a commercial landlord on repudiation.

Why it comes up in mediation

A tenant who believes walking away caps the exposure at a few months of rent is often wrong by an order of magnitude. Knowing the real number early is usually what makes a surrender or restructured lease possible.

Hryniak v. Mauldin
2014 SCC 7
What happened

Investors sued over funds placed with an investment scheme. The question on appeal was when a court may decide a case by summary judgment rather than sending it to trial.

What the court decided

A culture shift is required toward timely and affordable access to civil justice. Summary judgment should be granted where it allows the judge to reach a fair and just determination on the merits, which will be so where the process allows the judge to find the necessary facts, apply the law, and is a proportionate, more expeditious and less expensive means to achieve a just result.

The rule

Many disputes can be decided without a trial where the record permits a fair determination.

Why it comes up in mediation

Parties routinely describe their alternative to settlement as years of litigation. Sometimes the real alternative is a motion decided in months. That single correction changes the value of holding out.

Family and estate

Co-ownership and partition, constructive trust, and contribution without title. Separation, divorce and parenting are accredited family mediation and are not covered here.

Latcham v. Latcham
2002 CanLII 44960 (ON CA)
What happened

Co-owners of property disagreed about sale, in a context where family law claims were also in play.

What the court decided

A co-owner has a strong prima facie right to partition or sale, and the discretion to refuse is narrow. But where a legitimate family law claim would be unfairly prejudiced by an immediate sale, a partition application should generally not be granted while that claim is outstanding.

The rule

The right to force a sale of jointly owned property is strong, but it is not absolute, and competing family law claims can displace it.

Why it comes up in mediation

Both halves matter at the table. The co-owner who insists they can block a sale indefinitely is usually wrong. The co-owner who assumes a sale is automatic may also be wrong. Establishing which situation this is tends to reset both positions at once.

Soulos v. Korkontzilas
[1997] 2 SCR 217
What happened

A real estate broker acting for a buyer negotiated for a commercial building, then bought it himself through his wife without telling his client. The property did not increase in value, so the client could show no conventional financial loss.

What the court decided

A constructive trust may be imposed to remedy a breach of an equitable obligation such as a fiduciary duty, even where there has been no enrichment corresponding to a deprivation in the usual sense. Maintaining the integrity of fiduciary relationships is itself a good reason for the remedy.

The rule

A proprietary remedy can follow a breach of loyalty, not only a provable financial loss.

Why it comes up in mediation

Disputes involving agents, advisers and partners often proceed on the assumption that no measurable loss means no claim. That assumption is unreliable where a duty of loyalty was owed.

Kerr v. Baranow
2011 SCC 10
What happened

Two appeals concerning property claims between unmarried partners after long relationships, where contributions had been made without title reflecting them.

What the court decided

Unjust enrichment requires an enrichment, a corresponding deprivation, and the absence of a juristic reason for the enrichment. Where the parties' efforts amount to a joint family venture and there is a link between contributions and accumulated wealth, a proportionate monetary award may be calculated on a value-survived basis rather than as a fee for services.

The rule

Contribution without title can found a claim, and the remedy is not limited to an hourly value of work performed.

Why it comes up in mediation

Included here for the framework rather than the domestic context. Registered title frequently does not describe the whole economic arrangement between siblings, family members or business partners who put money and work into an asset.
