Strategies

The things that actually move a dispute.

Almost nobody is stuck because they do not know the law. They are stuck because of how the problem was framed, what each side assumes about the other, and what nobody has put on the table yet. Here is the method I use to change that, in full, so you can see it before deciding whether to use it.

Use this whether or not you ever hire me. None of it is secret or complicated. If you apply it yourself and settle at the kitchen table without paying anyone, that is a good outcome. Do that.

Five things · settle these first

Before you sit down

Worth settling in your own head first. They do more for the outcome than anything that happens on the day.

01

Know what happens if nothing is agreed

Most people measure a settlement against their best day in court. Take four subtractions off that: the likely result rather than the best one, the legal fees to get there, the year or two it takes, and whether the other side can actually pay when the day comes.

The question

If we never agree, what happens to me, by when, and what is left in my hand after costs?

02

Separate what you are asking for from what you need

A number is usually standing in for something else. Certainty. An exit. A date. Being taken seriously after months of being ignored. Name the real need and there are usually several ways to meet it. Only one of them was on the table.

The question

If I got the money but not the apology, would I take it? If I got the date but less money, would I take that?

03

Know what you can actually perform

An agreement you cannot carry out is worse than none. You are back here in six months, having also spent the goodwill. It is the most common reason signed settlements fall apart.

The question

What can I do, by when, without needing anything else to go right first?

04

Put a number on staying stuck

A dispute costs you every month even when nothing happens: carrying costs, interest, a property you cannot use or sell, professional fees, and the attention it eats. Add it up. It is often bigger than the gap between the two positions.

The question

What is this costing me every month, and how many more months am I prepared to pay that?

05

Decide in advance what you will not trade

Set your limit before the room, not while someone sits waiting for an answer. A limit set calmly is one you can hold without drama, and it makes everything above it genuinely negotiable.

The question

What is the one thing I will walk away over, and is it actually that, or is it pride?

Six traps · all of them ordinary

Why sensible people stay stuck

None of these are stupidity. Smart, well-advised people do all six. Seeing them named is usually enough to break their grip.

01

Assuming there is one pot to split

The costliest assumption is that the pot is fixed, so any gain for them is a loss for you. Some disputes are one number. Most property disputes are not: timing, possession, condition, repairs, chattels, carrying costs, releases, confidentiality. Each is worth more to one of you than the other. That is what makes a trade possible.

The question

What is on this table that I care about less than they do, and what do they hold that I want more than they think?

The “mythical fixed pie,” Program on Negotiation, Harvard Law School
02

It is mine, so it is worth more

People value what they own above what anyone will pay for it. That is measured, and ordinary, not a character flaw. It is why two honest co-owners can look at the same house and be a hundred thousand dollars apart without either one lying.

The question

Am I valuing this at what it is worth, or at what it would cost me to give it up?

The endowment effect
03

Being certain about what a court would do

Both sides have been told they have a strong case, and both believe it. They cannot both be right. The gap between two confident predictions is usually the whole reason the file is still open.

The question

What would have to be true for the other side to win, and how sure am I that it is not?

04

Confusing being right with being paid

A decision in your favour says you are owed something. Turning that into money is a second project, with its own cost and timeline, and it only works if there is something to collect from.

The question

If I win, who writes the cheque, and out of what?

05

Spending because you have already spent

Say you have spent $40,000 in legal fees. That money is gone whether you settle this afternoon or fight for two more years, because winning recovers only part of your costs, and only if they can pay. What you have spent is a reason to finish this properly. It is not a reason to spend the next $40,000 the same way.

The question

If this landed on my desk today, exactly as it stands, and I had not spent a dollar, would I choose to spend the next $40,000 fighting it?

06

Discounting an offer because of who made it

One set of terms, sent by the other side’s lawyer, reads as an insult. The identical terms from a neutral get considered. Nothing changed but who said it. That is much of why a third person in the room changes what is acceptable without changing what is on offer.

The question

If a friend I trusted had suggested this exact deal, would I still be saying no?

Nine moves · in the order they happen

What we do in the room

The actual method, start to finish. You are entitled to know exactly what you are walking into.

01

The same read, given to both of you

Before the session you both receive the same document: the money, the timing, what the market says, the cost of continuing, and the exposure on each side. Nobody gets a version written for them. It is the fastest way to stop each of you negotiating against a picture only you can see.

02

Private sessions

Each side also meets me alone. This is where people say what they will not say across the table, including what they would actually accept. It stays with me. Nothing leaves your private session unless you tell me to take it.

03

Reality testing

Not telling you that you are wrong, and not arguing. Asking the one question your position rests on, then waiting while you answer. This all depends on a judge agreeing the deadline was waived. What is your plan for the month it does not? Uncomfortable for a minute, then the most useful minute of the day.

04

Widening the table

When one number is stuck, add issues instead of splitting it further. Closing date, possession, what stays in the house, repairs, carrying costs, who lists it, a release, confidentiality. You value these differently, so giving up what you care least about buys what you care most about. Splitting one number cannot do that.

05

Pricing the disagreement instead of arguing it

You say the house is worth $900,000. They say $780,000. Neither can prove it today, and two appraisals will not settle it. Or you agree this afternoon that it goes on the market and the split is calculated on what it actually sells for, with a floor if the market drops. The argument becomes a line in the agreement instead of the obstacle.

The contingent contract, Program on Negotiation, Harvard Law School
06

Brackets, when the numbers stall

When two positions stop moving, a bracket restarts them: I will come to X if they come to Y. Neither side concedes alone, both signal a range they can live in, and the conversation moves from two fixed points to a zone.

07

Structure before size

A smaller amount that is secured, staged, or paid next month is often worth more than a larger amount backed by nothing. Someone who cannot write one cheque can often write six. Here, knowing the property and the market matters more than knowing the law.

08

A proposal from me, only at the end

If everything else is exhausted and the gap is small, I can put one proposal to both sides. It is governed: only if you both agree in advance to receive it, you each answer privately, no deal unless you both accept, and neither is told what the other said. Nobody is exposed by saying yes to something the other side refused.

Adapted from the “No One Gets Hurt” protocol, Mitchell Rose, ADR Institute of Canada
09

Writing it down before anyone leaves

Agreements that are not recorded come apart within the week, because each side remembers the version that favours them. Whatever is agreed gets written the same day, in plain terms, and signed.

15 questions · take them with you

Questions worth asking

These work in any negotiation, with or without me in the room.

Ask yourself

  • What do I actually need here, as distinct from what I have been asking for?
  • If there is no agreement at all, what happens to me, and when?
  • What is this costing me every month while it is unresolved?
  • What can I genuinely deliver, and by when?
  • What would have to be true for the other side to be right?
  • What is the smallest version of this I could accept and still feel finished?

Ask the other side

  • What does this need to look like for you to sign it?
  • Which part matters most to you, and which part could move?
  • What can you actually perform, as opposed to what you would like to offer?
  • What are you worried about that we have not talked about?
  • If the money were right, what would still be unresolved between us?

Ask about any proposed deal

  • Can we both actually do this, without anything else having to go right?
  • What happens if one of us does not perform, and what backs it up?
  • Does this close everything between us, or leave something alive?
  • Does any part of this depend on a number landing a certain way, and have we said what happens if it does not?

What I do not do

I do not decide anything and I do not take a side. I act for neither of you and give no legal advice. I will not push a settlement because it would be tidy, and you can end the process at any point.

I also never take the listing, the sale, or any brokerage work arising out of a dispute I mediate. That is a rule, not a preference, and you can hold me to it.
