Strategies

The things that actually move a dispute.

Almost nobody is stuck because they do not know the law. They are stuck because of how the problem has been framed, what each side assumes about the other, and what nobody has put on the table yet. This is the method I use to change that, written out in full so you can see it before you decide whether to use it.

Use this whether or not you ever hire me. None of it is secret and none of it is complicated. If you read this page, apply it yourself, and settle your dispute at the kitchen table without paying anyone, that is a good outcome and you should do that.

This is general information about a process. It is not advice about your situation, and it is not legal advice.

Five things · settle these first

Before you sit down

Worth deciding in your own head before the day. These do more for the outcome than anything that happens in the room.

01

Know what actually happens if nothing is agreed

Most people measure a settlement against their best day in court. The honest comparison sits three subtractions below that: the likely result rather than the best one, minus the legal fees to get there, minus the year or two it takes. Then one more subtraction that people forget entirely, which is whether the other side will actually be able to pay it when the day comes.

The question

If we never agree, what happens to me, by when, and what is left in my hand after costs?

02

Separate what you are asking for from what you need

A number is usually standing in for something else. Certainty. An exit. A date. Being taken seriously after months of being ignored. When the real need is named, there are often several ways to meet it, and only one of them was on the table.

The question

If I got the money but not the apology, would I take it? If I got the date but less money, would I take that?

03

Know what you can actually perform

An agreement you cannot carry out is worse than no agreement, because you will be back here in six months having also spent the goodwill. This is the single most common reason settlements fail after they are signed.

The question

What can I do, by when, without needing anything else to go right first?

04

Put a number on staying stuck

Being in a dispute has a monthly cost even when nothing is happening: carrying costs, interest, a property you cannot use or sell, professional fees, and the part of your attention it takes up. People rarely add it up, and it is often larger than the gap between the two positions.

The question

What is this costing me every month, and how many more months am I prepared to pay that?

05

Decide in advance what you will not trade

Know your limit before you are in the room, not while someone is looking at you waiting for an answer. A limit you set calmly in advance is one you can hold without drama, and it makes everything above it genuinely negotiable.

The question

What is the one thing I will walk away over, and is it actually that, or is it pride?

Six traps · all of them ordinary

Why sensible people stay stuck

None of these are stupidity. Smart, well-advised people do all six. They are well documented, and simply seeing them named is usually enough to break their grip.

01

Assuming there is one pot to split

The most destructive assumption people bring is that the resources are fixed, so any gain for the other side is a loss for you. Some disputes really are a single number. Most property disputes are not: they carry timing, possession, condition, repairs, chattels, who carries what until it sells, releases, and confidentiality. Each of those is something one side values more than the other, which is exactly what makes a trade possible.

The question

What is on this table that I care about less than they do, and what do they hold that I want more than they think?

The “mythical fixed pie,” Program on Negotiation, Harvard Law School
02

It is mine, so it is worth more

People consistently value what they own above what anyone else will pay for it. This is measured and it is ordinary, not a character flaw. It is why two honest co-owners can look at the same house and be a hundred thousand dollars apart without either of them lying.

The question

Am I valuing this at what it is worth, or at what it would cost me to give it up?

The endowment effect
03

Being certain about what a court would do

In most disputes both sides have been told they have a strong case, and both believe it. They cannot both be right. That gap between two confident predictions is usually the whole reason the matter is still open.

The question

What would have to be true for the other side to win, and how sure am I that it is not?

04

Confusing being right with being paid

A decision in your favour is a finding that you are owed something. Turning that into money is a separate project with its own cost and timeline, and it only works if there is something on the other end to collect from.

The question

If I win, who writes the cheque, and out of what?

05

Spending because you have already spent

Say you have spent $40,000 in legal fees getting to here. That $40,000 is gone. It is gone if you settle this afternoon and it is gone if you fight for another two years, because even winning recovers only a portion of your costs, and only if the other side can pay them. What you have already spent is a reason to want this finished properly. It is not a reason to spend the next $40,000 the same way.

The question

If this dispute landed on my desk today, exactly as it stands, and I had not spent a dollar yet, would I choose to spend the next $40,000 fighting it?

06

Discounting anything because of who proposed it

Take one set of terms. Send it in an email from the other side’s lawyer and people call it insulting. Put the identical terms in front of them from a neutral and they will think about it. Nothing changed but who said it. This is a large part of why having a third person in the room changes what is acceptable without changing what is actually on offer.

The question

If a friend I trusted had suggested this exact deal, would I still be saying no?

Nine moves · in the order they happen

What we do in the room

The actual method, start to finish. Nothing here is secret, and you are entitled to know exactly what you are walking into.

01

The same read, given to both of you

Before the session, both sides receive one identical document setting out the situation as it looks from outside it: the money, the timing, what the market says, the costs of continuing, and the exposure on each side. Nobody gets a version written for them. It is the fastest way to end the state where each side is negotiating against a picture only they can see.

02

Private sessions

Each side also meets with me alone. This is where people say the thing they will not say across the table, including what they would actually accept. It stays with me. Nothing from your private session goes to the other side unless you tell me to take it there.

03

Reality testing

Not telling you that you are wrong, and not arguing with you. Asking the one question your whole position rests on, and then waiting while you answer it. All of this depends on a judge agreeing the deadline was waived. Your lawyer thinks that is likely. What is your plan for the month it goes the other way? It is uncomfortable for about a minute, and then it is the most useful minute of the day. I do it to both sides, equally hard.

04

Widening the table

When a single number is stuck, the move is usually to add issues rather than to keep splitting the one. Closing date, possession, what stays in the house, repairs, who carries the property until it sells, who lists it, a release of everything else between you, and confidentiality. The reason this works is simple: you each care about these things by different amounts, so handing over the one you care least about can buy the one you care most about. Splitting a single number can never do that.

05

Pricing the disagreement instead of arguing it

You say the house is worth $900,000. They say $780,000. Neither of you can prove it today, and you can spend a year and two appraisals trying. Or you can agree this afternoon that it goes on the market and the split is calculated on whatever it actually sells for, with a floor underneath so nobody is ruined if the market drops. You never have to settle the argument. It stops being the obstacle and becomes a line in the agreement.

The contingent contract, Program on Negotiation, Harvard Law School
06

Brackets, when the numbers stall

When two positions have stopped moving, a bracket restarts them: I will come to X if they will come to Y. Neither side concedes unilaterally, both signal a range they can live in, and the conversation moves from two fixed points to a zone.

07

Structure before size

A smaller amount that is secured, or staged, or paid next month, is frequently worth more than a larger amount backed by nothing. Someone who cannot write one cheque can often write six. This is where knowing the property and the market matters more than knowing the law.

08

A proposal from me, only at the end

If everything else is exhausted and the gap is small, I can put a single proposal to both sides. It is governed: I only do it if both of you agree in advance to receive it, you each answer me privately, there is no deal unless you both accept it, and neither of you is told what the other said. Nobody is exposed by saying yes to something the other side rejected.

Adapted from the “No One Gets Hurt” protocol, Mitchell Rose, ADR Institute of Canada
09

Writing it down before anyone leaves

Agreements reached and not recorded tend to come apart within the week, because each side remembers the version that favours them. Whatever is agreed is written the same day, in plain terms, and signed.

15 questions · take them with you

Questions worth asking

These work in any negotiation, with or without me in the room.

Ask yourself

  • What do I actually need out of this, as distinct from what I have been asking for?
  • What happens to me if there is no agreement at all, realistically, and when?
  • What is this costing me every month while it is unresolved?
  • What can I genuinely deliver, and by when?
  • What would have to be true for the other side to be right?
  • What is the smallest version of this I could accept and still feel finished?

Ask the other side

  • What does this need to look like for you to be able to sign it?
  • Which part of this matters most to you, and which part could move?
  • What can you actually perform, as opposed to what you would like to offer?
  • What are you worried about that we have not talked about?
  • If the money were right, what else would still be unresolved between us?

Ask about any proposed deal

  • Can both sides actually do this, without anything else having to go right?
  • What happens if one of us does not perform, and what backs it up?
  • Does this close everything between us, or does it leave something alive?
  • Is there anything here that only works if a number lands a certain way, and have we said what happens if it does not?

What I do not do

I do not decide anything, and I do not take a side. I do not act for either of you, and I give no legal advice to anyone. I do not push a settlement because it would be tidy, and you are free to end the process at any point.

I also never take the listing, the sale, or any brokerage work arising out of a dispute I mediate. That is a rule, not a preference, and you can hold me to it.
