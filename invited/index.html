You have been pointed here because the other side of a disagreement involving you has asked whether it can be resolved directly, with a neutral in the room, instead of through a court. This page explains what that is, what it is not, and what happens if you would rather not.

Read this before you decide anything. You are not required to do anything, you are not agreeing to anything by reading it, and nothing here is a claim against you.

Straight answers

Who are you, and who do you act for?

My name is Taran Aujla. I am a mediator. I act for neither side. I have not been hired to represent the person who contacted me, I am not their lawyer, and I am not going to argue their case to you.

A mediator is a neutral. My job is to see whether there is an agreement available that both of you would rather have than the alternative. If there is not, I will say so.

Does this mean I am being sued?

No. This is the opposite of that. Someone on the other side of a disagreement with you has asked whether it can be worked out directly instead of through a court.

Nothing has been filed because of this invitation, and nothing is filed by you replying to it.

What would I actually be agreeing to?

At this stage, a conversation. Nothing more.

If you both decide to go ahead, there is a written agreement to mediate that sets out how the process runs before anything else happens. Nothing is binding on you unless you sign it. I do not decide anything, I cannot impose an outcome, and you can stop at any point.

What does it cost, and who pays?

A fixed fee, agreed in writing before anything begins, normally split between the two sides. You will know the number before you commit to anything.

Compare that to what each side spends on a contested matter, and the arithmetic is usually the least difficult part of this decision.

Is this confidential?

Largely, and I would rather be precise than reassuring. What is said in a mediation is treated as without-prejudice settlement discussion, so it is not evidence against you on the merits if the matter later goes to court.

Three things can still surface. A judge fixing costs at the end of a case may consider written offers and how a party conducted itself. A settlement you sign is a binding contract and can be enforced. And a lawyer’s conduct remains subject to their own professional rules. The written agreement to mediate can narrow the first of those, and I raise it with both sides before we start.

Should I get my own lawyer?

I recommend that each side get independent legal advice, and I say that to both of you equally. I am neutral, which means there is nobody in the room whose job is to tell you what a deal is worth to you.

You are not required to have one. Plenty of people mediate without. If you do have a lawyer, keep them and bring them in.

What if I just say no?

Then nothing happens, and I will not contact you again about it.

Saying no is not an admission of anything and it does not put you at a disadvantage. The other side’s options are exactly what they were before I got in touch. You are also free to say not yet, or to ask me questions first without committing to anything.

Why the invitation came from me and not from them

Because an offer to talk lands differently depending on who makes it. The same suggestion from the other side of a dispute often reads as a tactic. From someone who is not on either side, it is easier to consider on its merits.

That is the only reason. Nobody is managing you.

What happens next

01

You ask me anything

A short call, no charge and no obligation. Most people want to know who I am and why I am involved before anything else. That is a reasonable place to start.

02

You decide

If it is not for you, say so and that is the end of it. If it is, both sides sign the agreement to mediate and I set out the process in writing.

03

One focused session

Online, usually within a few weeks. If it resolves, it is written up and signed the same day. If it does not, nothing you said is held against you.
