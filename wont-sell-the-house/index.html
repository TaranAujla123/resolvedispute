The part most people do not know

Under section 2 of Ontario’s Partition Act, a co-owner — joint tenant or tenant in common — has a prima facie right to compel partition or sale, and the other co-owners have a corresponding obligation to permit it. It is not absolute. A court keeps a discretion and can refuse where the person asking is acting maliciously, vexatiously or oppressively, and it will consider anyone living there.

The practical effect is that the person refusing usually cannot simply refuse forever. Which means the real question is not whether it gets resolved. It is on whose terms, and how much of the value survives the process.

Get your own lawyer to tell you how that applies to your situation. It will apply differently depending on the facts.

What it is actually about

Almost never the price. Underneath, in rough order of how often it comes up:

  • One of you has been living there and the other has been paying.
  • One did the work on it and the other watched the value go up.
  • A parent said something years ago that everyone remembers differently.
  • One of you has a spouse driving this who is not in the room.
  • Somebody feels they were treated as though they did not count.

None of that is in a statement of claim, and none of it gets resolved by one. But it is usually what is actually holding the thing up.
